Research

Why lead-free perovskites?

The best perovskite solar cells still contain lead, and that is a big reason they are not on rooftops yet. Tin-based perovskites are safer, but they still lag behind lead-based cells in efficiency and stability. My work is about closing that gap.

Tin-based perovskite solar cells

Films and devices are processed in an inert-atmosphere glovebox by spin coating and thermal evaporation, then characterized with FESEM, XRD and UV-Vis spectroscopy.

How it works

How a perovskite solar cell turns light into current

01 · Light enters

Sunlight reaches the absorber

Photons pass through the glass and the transparent electrode (ITO) and reach the perovskite layer, which is only a few hundred nanometres thick.

02 · Absorption

Each photon creates an electron–hole pair

A photon with more energy than the band gap lifts an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving a positive hole behind. Tin perovskites have band gaps of about 1.3 to 1.4 eV, close to the ideal for a single-junction cell.

03 · Separation

Selective layers pull the charges apart

The electron transport layer accepts only electrons, and the hole transport layer accepts only holes. Each pair is split before it can recombine and waste its energy as heat.

04 · Extraction

Electrons do work in the circuit

Electrons collected at the metal electrode flow through the external circuit, power the load and return to the transparent electrode, where they meet the holes. That flow is the photocurrent.

05 · The tin challenge

Why tin is hard, and where my work comes in

Tin(II) oxidizes easily to tin(IV) in air, creating defects that trap charges before they reach the contacts. That is why my cells are made in an oxygen-free glovebox, and why tin cells still trail lead-based ones.

Solar cells without lead have to work as well as the ones with it. I build them, measure them, and *model* *what* *happens* *inside.*
